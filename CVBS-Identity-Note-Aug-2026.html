The identity question, answered honestly

Whether a rebrand is the right response to the sameness talk, what is genuinely wrong with the current mark, and the one change that is worth more than a new logo.

The verdict, before the pretty pictures

The short answer

A logo overhaul is not what Dion's talk implies, and presenting one on Wednesday would work against you.

His entire argument is that sameness is a positioning and operations problem, not a design problem. Reputation is built from repeated behaviour, not from a mark. A business with a clear position and a plain logo beats a business with a beautiful logo and no position, every time.

But there is one real brand problem here, and it is not the gradient. It is that CVBS leads with an acronym nobody can say, spell or search for, while owning one of the most valuable exact-match names in the category. That is worth fixing, and it is worth more than any mark.

There is also a tactical reason not to open with a rebrand on Wednesday. You are about to present a website. If you present a new logo in the same meeting, you have told the room that the site you just built sits on the wrong brand. That is a self-inflicted wound in the one meeting where you need the work to feel finished.

Raise the principle. Show one slide. Price it separately. Details at the end.

Diagnosis

What is actually wrong with the current mark

Current mark, on light

Five faults, in order of how much they cost you.

1  The second line reads as a word

Stacked, it is CV above BS. Once somebody sees it, they cannot unsee it, and nobody in the room will tell you. This alone is enough reason to stop stacking it, and it is the single most important sentence in this document.

2  It says software company, not hospitality

A cyan to blue gradient, chamfered corners and a heavy underline bar is the visual language of a 2010s IT services or telco brand. Cover the name and this could be a managed services provider, a payments gateway or a fleet tracking company. Nothing about it says venues, rooms, hosting or thirty seven years of relationships.

In Dion's terms this is a pattern contradiction. Your position is four named people who have been in the room. Your mark says anonymous technology vendor.

3  The gradient does not survive contact with the real world

A gradient fails the moment it leaves a screen: one colour print, a supplier portal that strips images, an embroidered shirt, a stamp, a fax-quality PDF a venue's contracts team sends back, a 16 pixel browser tab. Look at the smallest version above. Every mark you will ever need has to work in one flat colour first, and then earn its colour.

4  The acronym carries no meaning and no search value

CVBS means nothing to an event organiser who has not met you. It is four letters they cannot say out loud, cannot spell back to a colleague, and will not type into anything. Meanwhile you own conferencevenues.com, which is the exact phrase your buyer actually searches, and it is hidden behind the acronym.

5  The underline bar

It adds nothing, eats the clear space, and is the first thing to break at small sizes. Minor, but it is there.

The recommendation

Lead with the words. Retire the acronym.

This is the change worth making, and it is not really a design decision. It is a naming decision with a design consequence.

Trade as Conference Venues. Keep Conference Venues and Booking Services as the legal name, in the footer and on the invoice. Retire the CVBS monogram from the primary position and let it live on as an app icon and a favicon, where four letters are all that fit.

The honest counterargument

"Conference Venues" is generic. You cannot trademark it, and Dion would say a generic name is the sameness problem in its purest form. That objection is correct and you should expect Karen to raise it.

The answer: distinctiveness comes from the position and the mark, not from the name. Being generic is a liability when the name is all you have. It is an asset when you already own the domain, thirty seven years of it, and a position nobody else can claim. General Motors, British Airways and Standard Chartered are all generic. What makes them findable is that nobody else can be them.

And for the being-quoted work already underway, an entity called Conference Venues is worth considerably more than an entity called CVBS. Assistants match on meaning. "CVBS" means nothing to them either.

The mark

Three directions, and the one I would build

Every venue finder in the world uses the same four devices: a map pin, a globe, a building silhouette, or an abstract swoosh. Put six of them on a page and you have Dion's sameness wall in logo form. So the test is not "is it nice". The test is whether an event organiser could recognise it with the name covered.

Direction one: the room. Recommended.

A room in plan view, with the doorway open and someone standing just inside it. It is the thing CVBS actually deals in, drawn the way a planner already reads it, and it is the position stated in a single shape: we have walked into this room.

Why the room wins

It holds at sixteen pixels, works in one colour, is not a pin, a globe or a building, and it is the only one of the three that says something rather than depicting something. The dot inside the doorway is the whole position: a person, in the room, before you get there.

Colour

Kill the gradient. Keep the navy. Grow up the teal.

The palette does not need replacing. It needs one deletion and one adjustment.

Out: the gradient, and the cyan that drives it

The best idea in here

The stamp, which is not a logo

The most distinctive device available to CVBS is not a mark at all. It is a stamp that says a person has been in the room, applied to the one artefact the buyer actually forwards to their boss.

On a shortlist, beside each venue

It is worthy of remark in Dion's exact sense: not spectacle, just unusually specific in a category where everybody is vague. It is impossible to fake without the underlying practice. It is free, because the visits already happen. And it lands on the shortlist document, which reaches the decision maker more reliably than any page on the website.

The same device is already wired into the venue index and prints the moment Karen fills in the site visit log.

Do not put this in the logo

The temptation will be to make the stamp the identity. Resist it. A stamp is a device that carries variable information, which is exactly what a logo must never do. Keep the mark constant and let the stamp change.

Next

How to handle it on Wednesday

Do not present a rebrand. Present the site. The identity work is a separate conversation with a separate fee, and raising it as part of the website presentation devalues both.

  1. One slide, near the end, one minute. Show the current mark at sixteen pixels beside the words "conferencevenues.com". Ask which one an organiser would remember. Do not show a new logo.
  2. Say the CV over BS thing out loud. Once, plainly, without making it a joke. It is the only piece of information in this document they cannot get anywhere else, and somebody should have told them years ago.
  3. Offer the naming decision as the small first step. Trading as Conference Venues costs nothing and changes the search and citation picture immediately. It can be agreed in the room.
  4. Hold the mark back. If they want it, it is a scoped identity project: mark, wordmark, palette discipline, the stamp device, a one page usage sheet and the asset set. Price it on its own.
If you only take one thing

The logo is the least important part of this. The name is worth more than the mark, the stamp is worth more than the name, and the site visit log behind the stamp is worth more than all three. Spend the meeting on the log.
