What this project actually is

Three parts, working as one.

A website is where people land. It is not what brings them there, and it is not what keeps CVBS in mind between events. Social has been doing that job for a while now, and it will keep doing it. What changes from here is that all three point at the same place, so the work each one does adds to the other two instead of stopping where it lands.

What was holding it back

Six limitations, seen through a client's eyes.

01 · FIRST IMPRESSION

The scale of CVBS wasn't visible

The homepage opened on a dated template: a stock hero image, one button, and three short paragraphs. Nothing above the fold signalled an established, professional operation.

02 · SERVICE CLARITY

The service took work to understand

What CVBS does, that it costs the client nothing, and why it beats searching alone was spread across inner pages. A first-time visitor had to assemble the story themselves.

03 · VENUE DISCOVERY

There were no venues to discover

No destinations to browse, no venue pages, no way to move from "I need a venue in Sydney" to seeing what's possible. The site described a service, but never showed the product.

04 · TRUST

The proof was buried

Since 1989, the client list, the testimonials: all real, all persuasive, and almost all sitting on the About page rather than working for CVBS at every step.

05 · CONVERSION

Enquiring meant downloading a PDF

"Get a Quote" led to a downloadable PDF form, with the online version marked coming soon. Every extra step at the exact moment of intent costs enquiries.

06 · MOBILE

Mobile was an afterthought

The layout technically responded, but hierarchy, tap targets and enquiry pathways weren't designed for the device many organisers first reach for.

Who this is built for

Six audiences, and six pages already built for them.

These are not personas we invented. Each one already has its own page on the new site, and most of them are visible in the client list CVBS has built since 1989.

01 · SALES CONFERENCES

The annual kick-off

A hundred and fifty to five hundred people, dates locked a year ahead, and a room that has to hold energy for two days. Usually run by a marketing or events lead who wants real options quickly.

02 · MEDICAL AND PHARMACEUTICAL

Compliance comes first

Medtronic, Pfizer, Novo Nordisk and Bausch & Lomb are all in the client list. This audience works to rules about venue, catering and hospitality that most organisers never encounter, and needs someone who already knows them.

03 · ASSOCIATIONS

A committee that changes each year

Accommodation, meeting space, exhibition and catering, negotiated together, often for an organiser doing it for the first and only time.

04 · GOVERNMENT

Value has to be demonstrable

Not just felt. An independent shortlist, with rates negotiated on their behalf and the reasoning written down, is exactly the evidence a procurement process asks for.

05 · EXECUTIVE MEETINGS AND RETREATS

Small, and high stakes

Twenty senior people, where the room matters more than the rate. This is where knowing what a space actually feels like beats any filter on any platform.

06 · THE OCCASIONAL ORGANISER

Handed an event on top of a full job

The executive assistant or office manager who does this once or twice a year. They value a free expert more than anyone else does, and they become the most loyal clients CVBS has.

Different people, one problem. Too many venues, too little time, and no way to know whether a rate is fair.

The program

Twelve reasons to be in the inbox.

Nobody needs a venue finder until they suddenly need one urgently, and there is no way to predict which week that is. So the job of email is not to find the right person, it is to be there when the trigger fires with a ten second way to raise a hand. Two sends a month, every month, all ending on the same button.

Why the answer changes

Their shortlist is an algorithm. Yours is four people who have been there.

An algorithm can search a database. It cannot tell you which ballroom actually feels empty with a hundred and twenty people in it. Every platform in this market is racing to automate the part CVBS is best at, which is good news, because it turns the human part into the differentiator rather than the overhead.

Technology should make CVBS easier to find. It should not try to replace what makes CVBS worth finding. The advantage was always there. What was missing was somewhere to see it.

01 · WHO DOES THE WORK

Four people, not a filter

A platform matches a brief against a database. CVBS matches it against venues four people have walked through, eaten in and run events at. The About and Results pages now put those four on the screen with names and faces, which is the one thing a marketplace cannot copy.

02 · JUDGEMENT OVER VOLUME

Three venues, and the reason for each

An algorithm returns everything matching a filter and leaves the deciding to you. CVBS returns a recommendation and can say why, including what went wrong somewhere else last time. The site is built to show that, not to list rooms.

03 · BEING FOUND AT ALL

In the comparison, not after it

Marketplaces win by answering the question people actually type into Google. The venue index does the same for CVBS, city by city. The marketplaces publish capacities too. What they cannot publish is a capacity table with thirty-seven years of judgement sitting on the same page, which is the version an organiser trusts and an assistant quotes.

04 · STAYING HUMAN

A reply, not a notification

Half the emails ask for a reply rather than a click, and a real person answers it. A login cannot hold a relationship together across events that are years apart. Karen remembering the last one is worth more than any recommendation engine.
