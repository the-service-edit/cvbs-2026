Internal · not for the client

Presentation audit, and what to do before the room.

Thirty-five slides read four ways: every factual claim, every line of copy against the voice lock, every slide measured and screenshotted at four screen sizes, and the whole thing read once as Karen would read it.

19 August 2026 · CVBS-Website-Presentation · 35 slides

01The verdict

The deck is a beautiful account of what you made. It is not yet an account of what CVBS gets. Twenty of thirty-five slides describe a website Karen can open in a browser. None of them mentions money.

That is the whole finding. Everything below is either a defect I have already fixed or a consequence of that one gap. The craft is not in question and it will not be what decides the meeting. What decides the meeting is whether Karen can answer, out loud, three questions the deck currently leaves blank: what did the last twelve months of my money buy, what does this cost, and what happens if it does not work.

The second finding, and the more urgent one commercially: the deck as written arms her to cannibalise you. Four separate lines told her the website is finished, runs itself, is built once, and that social only got a facelift. Read in sequence they compose her counter-offer for her: pause the social for a year and put that money into the build. I have rewritten all four. The defence itself still needs building, and it is in section four.

Contrast

Roughly half of every headline in the deck was below the accessibility floor. The teal #28A8B6 on white measures 2.85:1 where 4.5:1 is required for text and 3:1 for large text, and on the stone background it drops to 2.60:1. On a projector, which is always worse than a monitor, the coloured half of your headlines was the hardest thing in the room to read.

Changed --teal-deep to #1B7480 (5.45:1 on white, 4.97:1 on stone) and --muted body copy to #666C74. It is the same teal family, deeper. If you hate it, it is one line in the CSS to revert. Screenshots are in the chat.

03The new slide: three channels, three jobs

Slide 21, opening the social section. Facebook and Instagram for the organisers, Karen's LinkedIn for the judgement, the CVBS page for the credential check.

This is the strongest new revenue argument in the deck and it is worth understanding why, because you should say it out loud in the room rather than letting the slide carry it.

It creates a new line without touching the old one

Instagram and Facebook are framed as continuing exactly as they are. The two LinkedIn channels are the part that is not running yet. That means the slide asks for more budget rather than reallocated budget, and it makes the existing retainer look like the foundation the new work sits on rather than the pot the new work could come out of. It is a quiet piece of anti-cannibalisation.

Karen's LinkedIn is the one thing a marketplace cannot copy, and it is currently a repost button

The whole competitive argument of slides 28 and 29 is that CVBS is four people who have stood in the room. Karen's personal LinkedIn is the only channel where that person actually speaks. Right now it mirrors Instagram. The slide names that plainly and offers the fix: you draft from what she already knows, she approves, it goes out in her name. She writes nothing.

The empty company page connects straight to the AI slides

The last bullet on the third column is "this is also the page an assistant reads when someone asks it whether CVBS is worth calling". That is deliberate. It ties the social section to slides 30 and 31 so the two are not read as separate asks, and it makes an empty LinkedIn page a visible commercial hole rather than a housekeeping item.

Your call The slide says Karen's LinkedIn "today it mirrors Instagram, and it is the one channel that should not". That is the only sentence in the deck that criticises something Karen does personally. It is true and it is the reason the slide works, but read it back and decide whether you want to say it or have it on screen.

04The four things that will cost you the meeting

None of these are fixable by editing a sentence. Ranked by what Karen does with them.

OneThere is no evidence in thirty-five slides

Not one follower count, one open rate, one click, one enquiry, one traffic figure. The words KPI, ROI and metric do not appear in the file. "Analytics" appears once, on slide 33, in the future tense.

Karen's first real question is "we already pay you $26,000 a year, what has that returned?" and the deck's own answer, on slide 22, was that it bought a look. She will ask it. There is nothing on screen to answer with.

What to do, and it is one hour of work: open Mailchimp and pull the open and click rate on Anthony's last Westin send. Open whatever analytics exist and pull one traffic number, however unflattering. Low is better for you, because low is the gap you are closing. Then look up monthly Australian search volume for "conference venues sydney" and "conference venue finder". If real demand exists and CVBS ranks nowhere, that single line turns the venue index from a content argument into a revenue argument. Send me whatever you find and I will build the slide.

TwoShe will say "let's pause the social to pay for the website"

She will say it warmly, as though she is solving your problem. The four lines that wrote it for her are now fixed, but the defence does not exist. Right now it lives in one clause on slide 32.

What to build: one slide, immediately after "what happens every month" and immediately before you name the number. Two columns of equal weight. Left, build it and keep the engine running. Right, pause the social to pay for the build, shaded in the same faded grey the deck already uses for the "before" flow on slide 7. The right column ends with: a better-looking website, and the same enquiries.

The line that lands it, in your own words in the room: the website is the shopfront, social and email are the street, and pausing the street to pay for the shopfront saves $26,000 and buys a shop nobody walks past.

Say the word and I will build it. It needs one number from you: what share of visits to the current site came from social or email last quarter.

ThreeKey person risk, and it is on screen in monospace

Slide 19's browser chrome reads the-service-edit.github.io/cvbs-2026. Karen is looking at her company's new website living on your personal GitHub, under your agency's name. She will ask who owns the site, the domain, the Mailchimp account, the Canva files that slide 22 promises her, and what happens to all of it if this ends. Then Anthony will ask the quieter version: what happens if you are unavailable for three months.

The deck does not acknowledge that either question exists. For a sole operator asking a thirty-seven-year-old family business for $68,000 in year one, this is the objection, and silence on it reads as not having thought about it.

One slide, one hour. Domain, hosting, repository, Mailchimp, Canva, analytics, all in CVBS's name, listed. Plus one line on what happens if you are not available. It is pure reassurance and it costs you nothing to give.

FourThe ask is a feeling

Thirty-five slides built to close $32,000 plus $36,000 a year end on "We would love to keep building it with you." There is no price, no options, no first step, no review point, no exit.

You chose no pricing deliberately and I am not arguing with that. But no pricing means Karen anchors on the only number in her head, which is the $3,000 she paid in 2012. And an open-ended commitment with no exit is exactly the shape of thing a family business defers.

Shrink the yes. Replace slide 33's twelve-month roadmap with ninety days: real dates, who does what including what Karen and Chantelle owe you, a fixed fee for the ninety days, one review meeting at day ninety, and CVBS's explicit right to stop there. Then show the twelve months as what happens if it works. She will not commit to $68,000 in the room. She will commit to ninety days, and ninety days that hit their numbers is a twelve-month client.

05The order is wrong

The single highest return in this document, and it costs thirty minutes with no new content.

The best slide in the deck is 28, the CVBS versus marketplaces versus doing-it-yourself table. It is the only place in thirty-five slides where you demonstrate work Karen could not have done herself, about her market, with a citation. It flatters her by making her four people the product. It is the reason to hire an agency rather than a web developer.

It sits at 28 of 35. Seventy-nine percent of the way in, after twenty slides of website architecture, at the point where attention is worst.

Open on it

Cover, then the competitive table, then the Westin email teardown (slide 25: "the reader leaves, books direct, and CVBS is not in the transaction", which is a live revenue leak happening this month), and only then "here is what we built about it".

The meeting stops being a design review and becomes a threat briefing with a solution attached. Every website slide after it becomes evidence instead of decoration.

07Voice: what is left

The self-criticism is fixed. What remains is a register problem, and it is systemic rather than line by line.

"Rather than" appears twenty times. That is the deck's verbal tic and it is the reason the whole thing reads like a comparison document instead of a conversation. "Actually" appears fourteen times, which reads defensive. "Every" thirty-seven times. Ten separate assertions of "since 1989" or "thirty-seven years".

Three headline patterns are each used five or more times. Number plus noun ("Three parts", "Six limitations", "Six audiences", "Six things", "Twelve reasons", "Three in four") ten times, by which point the audience is counting the counting. Negation flips ("A brochure, not a starting point", "Four people, not a filter", "A reply, not a notification") seven times, three of them on one slide. "Every X..." five times.

Twenty-two question marks. Slide 11 has seven rhetorical questions in a row, slide 14 has eight more. Even voiced as the organiser, at that density it is a device rather than Karen talking.

The deck talks about the website far more than it talks about CVBS's business

Count the sentences whose subject is a page, a template, a section or a send, against the sentences whose subject is an organiser or a venue. The first group wins comfortably. Slide 15 and slides 28 and 29 are the only stretches that consistently pass the across-the-table test, because they are about who is booking and why. They are the model for everything else.
