Nine ways to say the same thing.
Only one of them is worth changing for.

The recommendation: option 3.

"The right venue isn't found. It's known." One word changes, and the whole position changes with it. "Sourced" is the word CVBS uses about itself. It is a supplier's word, and every venue finder in the country claims it. "Known" is the word the buyer would use about the value they are actually getting, and it is the one thing a search engine, a directory and an AI answer cannot do at any price.

It also keeps the sentence shape the brand already owns, which was the reason to keep the original line in the first place. The socials, the deck and the brand spec all still work. Nothing has to be re-shot. And it points directly at the firsthand position, without making a claim the site cannot yet prove.

Everything below is presented so the team can see the sacrifice each option demands. The eyebrow, buttons, photograph and three proof lines are held constant in every version, so the only variable on screen is the headline and the paragraph under it.

The nine, and what each one costs
On a phone, where most of them will read it

Two-sentence headlines and long ones wrap to four or five lines here. This is the test that kills lines, not the desktop view.

The weakest part of this exercise

Every one of these lines is a promise about knowing venues firsthand, and the site can only prove it in Sydney. The venue index has a "seen" field on every venue and it is still empty. Until Karen fills the site visit log, options 2, 7 and 8 are writing cheques the site cannot cash. Pick a line the evidence can carry today, or fill the log first and then pick.

What a competitor would do

Options 4 and 6. "Your event deserves more than a search result" and "tell us about your event, we will tell you where it belongs" are lines any venue finder in Australia could put up tomorrow, because they claim nothing that has to be true. They read well and they defend nothing.

What feels generic

The "deserves more than" construction. It is the single most used shape in service marketing, it flatters the reader instead of telling them something, and it puts the competitor in the headline rather than CVBS. Mel's instinct underneath it is right, which is to speak to the organiser about their event. Options 3, 5 and 6 keep that instinct without borrowing the construction.

What would make it impossible to ignore

Not the headline. The proof line under it. "A search engine has never been in the room" becomes unanswerable the day the homepage can say who walked the room and when. That is a data problem, not a copy problem, and it is worth more than any of these nine sentences.
